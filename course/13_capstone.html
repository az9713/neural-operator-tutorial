Where you start

Step 01 of the kit, the smallest honest baseline, is done. Lab 01 run B: FNO on 1D viscous Burgers at the paper's specification, trained at 64 points, tested at 64, 128, 256, 1024, with a checked solver, a seed, and a results file (Module 2). The kit asked for normalized L2, rollout error, runtime, and seed; the rollout is the one item still open, and Module 7 specifies it. Lab 02 gives you the semiconductor target: a checked steady thermal solver for a 10 mm die with random power maps, and an FNO baseline on it (Module 6).

The plan, in the kit's five steps

  1. Baseline (done). Lab 01 run B. Add the rollout from Module 7's lab so the "one step versus whole trajectory" question has a number on your own data.
  2. A compatible two source curriculum. Burgers at ν = 0.1 (Lab 01) and 1D advection–diffusion \(u_t + c u_x = \nu u_{xx}\) with \(c \in [0.5, 2]\), \(\nu \in [0.02, 0.2]\), solved exactly in Fourier space with the Lab 01 initial condition law. Inputs: the initial field, the coefficients as constant channels, and an equation identifier as a one hot channel. Keep units and normalization explicit and identical across sources; do not mix incompatible variables into one channel tensor. 1000 trajectories per source.
  3. Transfer to a held out target. First target, a parameter shift: Burgers at ν = 0.02, which forms steeper fronts (check the spectrum; 1024 points may not be enough, and the solver's own check will tell you). Second target, a physics shift: the thermal die of Lab 02, 2D and elliptic. For the second target the 1D FNO cannot be reused as is; use a 2D FNO with the sources embedded as \(N \times 1\) fields, or state that the architecture change is itself part of the shift and compare against a 2D scratch model. Pretrain on the sources, fine tune on \(f \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 1.0\}\) of the target, compare with an equal capacity scratch FNO. This is arm C against arm A of Module 10's matrix.
  4. Physics only after the data baseline works. Arm D: Module 11's residual sweep on the thermal target. Report whether it improves out of distribution error or trades field accuracy.
  5. Make the negative result useful. If C does not beat A, vary one dimension at a time: shared grid, equation identifier present or absent, shared trunk versus separate heads, coefficient range, pretraining sample count. Arm B (joint multi task) separates interference from lack of shared structure. Record every cell, including the ones that fail.

Deliverable

One page, called the transfer map. A grid: rows are targets (Burgers ν = 0.02, thermal die), columns are data fractions, cells are "C beat A by x percent" or "no gain" or "C worse", with the seed range. Below it: the one dimension at a time table from step 5 for every cell that failed. Beside it: the error versus fraction plot with four arms and seed bands. Attach the results files. A negative transfer map with these controls is a result worth keeping; a positive one without them is not.

What not to submit. A single final time image. A speed up without the reference solver's hardware and resolution. A super resolution claim without the spectrum. An "AI learns physics" sentence.

After the capstone

The kit's semiconductor path: move from the square die to irregular geometry (a die with a heat spreader, a package with vias), coefficients tied to materials, and inverse design (place the blocks to minimize the peak). The right tools then are a mesh model of Module 6's kind and a finite element solver; FEniCSx on Windows means Docker or WSL2 (research_notes/repos.md:216). The physics after thermal: drift–diffusion, then EUV mask diffraction, which is Maxwell on nanometre features and has two unverified public papers to check first (lineage.md:329).
