Why this module exists

Two of this course's founding decisions meet here. One keeps Accelerated Understanding as a case study with an explicit evidence grade on every claim. The other anchors the course in semiconductors, where the public neural operator literature from the Anandkumar group is empty (research_notes/lineage.md:328-329).

Both need the same skill, and it is a skill rather than a disposition: reading a speed-up or accuracy claim and asking what was measured, against what, on which hardware, and by whom. That skill has a technique, and it is the next section.

How to read a speed-up, with the arithmetic

A speed-up is a ratio of two times, and both the numerator and the denominator are choices. Six of those choices have to match before the ratio means anything: the hardware on each side, the spatial resolution, the reference solver and its tolerance, the precision, the batch size, and above all the quantity being computed. A model that predicts one scalar per geometry and a solver that produces a full field are not doing the same job, and their ratio is not a speed-up.

Then there is amortization, which is where most published speed-ups quietly become weaker or stronger than they look. A surrogate costs offline time \(T_{\text{off}}\) to generate data and train, and thereafter costs \(T_{\text{mod}}\) per evaluation against the solver's \(T_{\text{ref}}\). It pays for itself only after

\[n^* = \frac{T_{\text{off}}}{T_{\text{ref}} - T_{\text{mod}}}\]

evaluations. Below \(n^*\), running the solver is faster in total, whatever the per-call ratio says.

The FNO paper's own Bayesian inverse problem is the model of how to report this, because it gives every term. One FNO evaluation takes 0.005 s against the pseudo-spectral solver's 2.2 s on a 256² grid, both on GPU. The MCMC chain needs 30,000 evaluations, so the FNO route takes 2.5 minutes and the solver route over 18 hours. The paper also counts the roughly 12 hours of data generation and training against the FNO instead of hiding it (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:159-172). Put those into the formula:

\[n^* = \frac{12 \times 3600\ \text{s}}{2.2 - 0.005\ \text{s}} = \frac{43{,}200}{2.195} \approx 19{,}700 \ \text{evaluations}.\]

So the break-even is about 19,700 calls, and the experiment used 30,000. The FNO wins, by a factor of about 1.5 on total time rather than the 440 the per-call ratio suggests. Both numbers are true and they answer different questions. If you needed 5,000 evaluations rather than 30,000, the honest recommendation would be to run the solver.

The two benchmark papers set the standard for saying this out loud. MeshGraphNets: "we find it hard to provide a 'true' hardware-agnostic performance comparison". PDEBench: "Whether or not this constitutes a fair comparison is surely debatable" (research_notes/pdf-mgn-pdebench.md:331).

The caveat column has a pattern in it. Every one of these results is real, every headline number is larger than the honest number, and the gap is never fraud: it is the difference between a per-call ratio and a total-cost ratio, or between a scalar and a field, or between a forecast and a control loop. That gap is what the ledger below is for.

Semiconductors: the anchor with no public literature

A search for neural operator work on semiconductor or chip physics from the Anandkumar group returned nothing. Two papers from other groups apply physics-informed operators to EUV lithography mask simulation: "Physics-Informed Neural Operator for Warm-Starting Background-Decomposed and Preconditioned PSFD: Enabling Scalable 3-D EUV Mask Simulation", and "Physics-informed neural networks and neural operators for a study of EUV electromagnetic wave diffraction from a lithography mask". Their arXiv identifiers were never verified, and must be before either is cited (research_notes/lineage.md:328-329). That is an open item, flagged here rather than papered over.

Meanwhile the company names chip design as its first market: physics modelling of chip performance under heat and materials constraints (company-episode.md:126-128; seed episode 20:27 to 22:58). Anandkumar names inverse lithography mask design and quantum-dot gate design as her inverse-design examples in the earlier episode (01:16:00 to 01:17:15, :81). So the market with the loudest claim has the thinnest public record, which is an awkward but useful fact: it means anything you build here is not competing with a published baseline, and also that you have no published baseline to check yourself against.

What this course does about it. It generates its own semiconductor data rather than waiting. Lab 02 in Module 6 solves steady heat conduction in a 10 mm silicon die with random rectangular power blocks, which is the simplest physics behind "faster chips that don't melt", using a finite-difference solver checked against an analytic solution to \(1.25\times10^{-5}\). Every parameter in it is a modelling choice made in this repository and is marked as such on that page. It is a first physics rather than the field: EUV mask diffraction is a Maxwell problem on nanometre features, drift-diffusion is a coupled nonlinear system, and neither has public neural operator data either.

Lab: write the evidence ledger for one application

LAB A reading task, one page, and the only lab in the course with no code in it.

Pick one row of the applications table and read the primary paper's experiment section and not its abstract. For every number in the row, record five things: what quantity was measured, what reference it was measured against, the hardware on both sides, whether offline cost is counted, and whether the number describes a field or a scalar. Where the paper gives \(T_{\text{off}}\), compute the break-even count \(n^*\) from the formula above and say how many evaluations the reported use case actually needs. Where it does not, say so, and say what the missing number would have to be for the claim to hold.

Then find the company or press statement that cites the same work, and grade the distance between the two. The deliverable is a table like the one above with one column added: "what I would need to see to raise the grade". That column is the useful output, and writing it is harder than it looks, because "more evidence" is not an answer and "a benchmark" is not either.

Exit test

You can now compute the break-even point of a surrogate, name the six things that must match before a speed-up ratio means anything, and write down for any claim the specific experiment that would change its grade.

  1. For the FNO Bayesian inverse problem, reconstruct the total time comparison including offline cost, derive the break-even count, and say at what number of evaluations you would advise running the solver instead.
  2. Explain why "26,000× faster for drag" and "700,000× faster for flow prediction" are not comparable statements, naming which of the six matching conditions each one fails.
  3. Take the company's seven claims and write, for each, the single public experiment that would move it from grade C to grade B. "A benchmark" is not an acceptable answer; name the systems, the baseline and the metric.
  4. The catheter row is the only one whose deliverable is a physical object. Say why that makes it stronger evidence than a larger speed-up number, and what it still does not show.
