Physics foundation models and the transfer question

This is the course's research module, and the one claim it exists to test: that a model pretrained on several physical systems needs less data on a new system than an equal-size model trained on that system alone. The public record supports a narrow version of that claim with exact numbers, and complicates the broad version with documented negative transfer. Both go here, followed by the experiment that would settle it for your own systems.

Why this module exists

The company's stated core finding is that "the shared model wins": a multi-physics model beats equal-parameter single-domain specialists (research_notes/company-episode.md:153). It has published no benchmark, and a Tech Times headline on 26 August 2026 says as much in its title (:123).

That is not a dead end, because the claim is not proprietary. Anandkumar's own co-authored paper on the subject, DPOT, is public, and so is the work of five other groups. The claim is testable from the published record, and this module reads those papers for the claim, not for the architecture. Module 5 supplied the reason to be careful: empirical risk minimization on a measure \(\mu\) leaves predictions under a different measure \(\nu\) entirely free, so "transfer works" is never a fact about a model on its own, only about a model and a pair of distributions.

The claim, made testable

Hypothesis. A model pretrained on systems \(S_1,\dots,S_k\) and fine-tuned on a fraction \(f\) of the data of a held-out system \(S_{k+1}\) reaches lower test error on \(S_{k+1}\) than an equal-parameter, equal-compute model trained from scratch on the same fraction, across several seeds, with the gap largest at small \(f\).

Alternative outcomes, all of which have been observed somewhere. The gap is zero, so no transfer. The gap is negative, so interference. The gap depends on which systems are in the pretraining set, so transfer is structure-dependent. Or the gap vanishes as \(f\to1\), so transfer is a small-data phenomenon only.

Three words in that hypothesis carry the whole burden. "Equal-parameter", because a larger pretrained model beating a smaller scratch model proves nothing about pretraining. "Held-out", because a system that appeared in pretraining is not a transfer target, it is a training set. And "fraction", because the claim is about data efficiency, so the result is the whole curve of error against \(f\) and never a single point on it. A paper reporting one number at one data fraction has not tested this hypothesis. The project kit's experiment matrix (../05_physics_foundation_model_study_kit.html) is this hypothesis with four arms, and the lab below is that matrix instantiated on this course's own data.

Two numbers from that table should be memorised, because they are the ones any argument about physics foundation models has to get past: Poseidon's 20 samples against FNO's 1024, and Subramanian's 64 examples against 8,000. They come from two different groups using two different architectures on two different problem families, and they agree on the order of magnitude. That is as close to independent replication as this literature currently gets.

Evidence against, or that complicates

The same literature contains its own counterexamples, and the papers report them instead of burying them, which should be said before the list.

  1. Multi-task pretraining hurts individual tasks before fine-tuning. MPP's authors write plainly that "the multi-task training does hurt performance on individual tasks". On shallow water, MPP-AViT-B reaches 0.00240 against a task-specific model's 0.00047, five times worse, and worse at every model scale they tested (foundation.md:16-24). Compressible Navier-Stokes at Mach 0.1 and 1.0 and diffusion-reaction do improve, so the effect is per-task and not uniform.
  2. Transfer is conditional on distribution shift, exactly as Module 5 predicts. Subramanian et al. report that under large out-of-distribution parameter shifts, zero-shot and few-shot performance is poor, that Helmholtz specifically "shows larger performance drops as we go OOD", and that the pretraining advantage plateaus once the fine-tuning set approaches the pretraining set size, at around \(2^{15}\) examples (:103-107). Note which system fails: Helmholtz is the non-smoothing, oscillatory one, which is also where Module 5's rate arguments do not apply.
  3. One multi-physics model does not dominate another. On CFDBench zero-shot, MPP-L reaches 0.00650 against DPOT-L's 0.00749, and DPOT's own authors concede "room left for improvement" (:56). If the shared representation were doing the work, the larger and more broadly pretrained model would not lose.
  4. The pretraining sets are narrower than the word "foundation" implies. MPP, DPOT and Poseidon all pretrain on fluid-dynamics-family PDEs plus a few elliptic problems. No paper in this survey trains one model across fluids, electromagnetism, solid mechanics and quantum systems and beats specialists on all four. The broadest attempt is a Mamba-backbone model (arXiv 2511.21861) trained on 12 datasets from The Well (the dataset release is arXiv 2412.00568), which reaches state of the art on 6 of the 12 domains with a 46 percent mean VRMSE reduction, and therefore is not the best on the other 6 (:166).
  5. Rollout remains a separate unsolved problem. DPOT's long Kolmogorov rollout is still 33.5 percent after pretraining, and arXiv 2509.02846 states that PDE foundation models "struggle with auto-regressive rollout performance, especially in out-of-distribution cases" (:163). Module 7's wall does not move because the model was pretrained.
  6. In-context operator learning has no baseline at all. ICON reaches under 6 percent error with one demonstration and about 2 percent with five, which sounds remarkable until you notice it reports no comparison against a fine-tuned or scratch-trained model. Its authors also state that a network trained on one ODE family "can hardly generalize... beyond" it (:80-86). An impressive number without a control arm is not evidence for the hypothesis in this module.

Verdict

The narrow claim holds. Pretraining on a family of related PDEs reduces downstream data requirements by one to two orders of magnitude in the low-data, near-distribution regime, and that is supported by multiple independent results with reproducible numbers.

The broad claim does not. One model across genuinely different areas of physics, beating equal-size specialists everywhere, has one partial result at 6 of 12 domains and several documented counterexamples, of which MPP's own shallow-water number is the clearest.

Anandkumar's own statement in the earlier episode is the narrow claim, not the broad one: a model cannot transfer to a regime with zero related data, but coupled-physics fine-tuning, such as heat plus stretching, needs far fewer samples than training from scratch (01:14:20 to 01:15:48, company-episode.md:82). The gap between that and the company's marketing is a gap in the marketing, not in her reasoning.

Weather is the one domain where a multi-source, multi-resolution model already beats the operational alternative on held-out data, and by a wide margin: GraphCast beats ECMWF HRES on 90 percent of 1,380 targets, and GenCast beats ENS on 97.2 percent of 1,320 targets (foundation.md:140-146). Weather is also the domain with the most data, the most uniform geometry, and the longest history of standardized evaluation, so it is the best case, not a typical one.

Lab: the experiment matrix, on systems you generated yourself

LAB Not yet run. This is the capstone's core, specified here so that Module 13 can refer to it.

The sources are Lab 01's Burgers generator at ν = 0.1, plus a second viscosity of 0.02 as a shifted regime, and a 1D advection-diffusion generator built the same way: \(u_t + cu_x = \nu u_{xx}\) is exact in Fourier space and takes about ten lines. The target is either Lab 02's die thermal operator, which is elliptic and two-dimensional and therefore a genuine change of physics, or, for a first pass, Burgers at the unseen viscosity, which is only a parameter shift. Running the parameter shift first is the right order: if transfer fails there, it will not succeed on the harder target and you have saved four hours.

ArmTrainingWhat it isolates
AScratch FNO on the target only, at each data fractionthe baseline curve, without which nothing else means anything
BMulti-task FNO on sources and target jointly, with an equation identifier channelinterference against sharing
CPretrain on the sources, then fine-tune on the target fractionthe transfer claim itself
DArm C plus a normalized PDE residual penalty during fine-tuningModule 11's question

Hold these constant across every cell: parameter count, optimizer budget in steps, normalization, solver fidelity, and evaluation grid. Use fractions \(f\in\{0.01,0.05,0.10,0.25,1.0\}\) of 1000 target samples, with at least three seeds per cell, and split by initial-condition family and coefficient regime and never by random frames, for the reason Module 8 gives. The deliverable is the plot of error against \(f\) for all four arms with seed bands, plus a one-page transfer map saying where C beat A and where it did not. A negative result with these controls in place is a result, and a more useful one than a positive result without them.

The project kit lists five failure modes to watch for. Aliasing mistaken for super-resolution, which Module 2's spectrum check catches. Target leakage through the split. An equation token that lets the model memorize which dataset it is looking at instead of sharing structure. A residual loss that quietly dominates the data loss. And reporting a single final-time image instead of rollout and invariant errors.

The cost on this machine is manageable. Each FNO run in Lab 01 took about 5 minutes, so 4 arms by 5 fractions by 3 seeds is 60 runs, roughly 5 hours, and less with early stopping on the small fractions. The thermal target at 64² costs more per run; Module 6 has its timing.

Exit test

You can now state the transfer hypothesis with the three controls that make it falsifiable, cite the strongest published result on each side with its numbers, tell two different failure modes apart, and design the arm that separates memorisation from sharing.

  1. State the transfer hypothesis with all three controls, and say for each control what a paper that omitted it could have claimed falsely.
  2. Name one published result for the hypothesis and one against it, with their numbers and their sources.
  3. Explain why MPP's shallow-water result and Subramanian's Helmholtz result are two different failure modes, and say which one Module 5's ERM argument predicts.
  4. Design the arm that distinguishes "the equation token lets the model memorize which dataset it is on" from "the model shares structure across systems", using only the four arms above and one modification.
  5. Weather is the strongest case for the broad claim. Give three properties of the weather domain that make it a best case and not a typical one.
