Scale: memory, modes, sharding, and the trillion context arithmetic

Where the company's numbers come from, what the public work reaches, and what one 4 GB GPU measures. The quadratic cost argument rules out vanilla attention at industrial grids; it does not rule out attention, and the foundation models that exist are attention based with patching or Fourier attention.

Why this module exists

The seed episode's title is "Trillion Token Context. No, Really". The company site claims up to 1 trillion parameters at training and 5 trillion tokens or context at inference; press repeats "5 trillion data points in a single prompt" with no benchmark (research_notes/company-episode.md:107-124). Anandkumar's own derivation in the earlier episode is short: physical data is 3D plus time, industrial scale starts near 1,000 grid points per dimension, so the context is "hundreds of billions to even a trillion", and "forget ever having a transformer for anything of this scale" (01:09:14 to 01:09:54, :42). This module does that arithmetic with units, then measures the constants.

The arithmetic

Points. A \(1000^3\) grid is \(10^9\) points per time step. With \(c\) channels and \(T\) steps it is \(10^9 cT\) numbers; \(c = 10\), \(T = 100\) gives \(10^{12}\), the trillion. In float32 that is 4 TB per sample before any activations.

Attention. Dense self attention over \(n\) tokens costs \(O(n^2 d)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory for the score matrix. With \(n = 10^9\) the score matrix alone has \(10^{18}\) entries, \(4 \times 10^{18}\) bytes in float32, about four million 1 TB drives. This is the sentence "all of the world's compute will not be enough", and it is correct for dense attention over points.

What it does not rule out. Attention over \(p\) patches or slices with \(p \ll n\): MPP and Aurora use patch tokens, DPOT uses Fourier attention, Transolver attends over learned slices, with cost linear in \(n\) and quadratic in \(p\). The proposition that attention is a neural operator layer (Module 6) says the kernel is dense; the fix is Module 3's four answers applied to that kernel. So the quadratic argument rules out one design, not the family.

FNO. One Fourier layer on \(n\) points costs \(O(n \log n)\) for the FFTs and \(O(k_{\max}^d d_v^2)\) for the multipliers (ICLR p.6, research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:58-61). Time is near linear; memory is linear in \(n\) for activations. The parameter count in 3D is \(k_{\max}^3 d_v^2\) complex numbers per layer: with \(k_{\max} = 16\), \(d_v = 32\), four layers, that is \(4 \times 4096 \times 1024 \times 8\) bytes, 128 MiB, against 8 MiB for the same in 2D. Modes, not points, drive the parameter memory, and a 3D FNO at useful \(k_{\max}\) is heavy before it sees any data.

The gap between the table and the claim: the largest public neural operator results are a 0.25° globe (about \(1.4 \times 10^6\) points per level), million point meshes, and 3D compressible flow at \(128^3\) with 100 samples (PDEBench). None is \(10^9\) points per step with \(10^{12}\) context. The mechanisms that would get there are in the table: factorized weights, hierarchy, sharding, and cheap attention. Whether they compose at that scale is the company's unpublished claim.

RTX 3050 Laptop GPU, batch 1, forward only, no gradients, float32, median of five passes (../labs/03_scale/results_scale.json; the file on disk was regenerated by the notebook check and its timings differ from this table by about 1 percent, run to run). Five readings. Memory is linear in points above 256²: about 1,050 bytes per grid point, which is 32 channels × 4 bytes × about 8 live activation copies. Time is near linear: 1024² costs 44 times 64² for 256 times the points, because small grids are launch bound at about 3 ms. Modes are cheap in activations and expensive in weights: going from 16 to 64 modes multiplies the parameters by 14.4 (0.6M to 8.7M) and adds 62 MiB of fixed memory, but at 1024² it adds only 1.3 ms and 6 percent memory. The 4 GB card holds the forward pass at 1024² with room to spare, and would not hold the training pass (activations for backward roughly triple the memory) at that grid; the thermal lab's first run proved this the hard way at 128² with a 200 sample batch.

The extrapolation. Linear memory from the 1024² row gives \(1050 \times 10^9\) bytes, about 978 GiB, for a batch 1 forward pass on \(1000^3\) points at 32 channels, before the 3D weights (128 MiB at \(k_{\max} = 16\)) and before any gradient. Time by \(n \log n\) from the same row: about 203 s per forward pass on this GPU. These are lower estimates: they assume the 2D kernel efficiency holds in 3D and ignore the memory of the FFT workspace at that size. So one time step of a trillion point field, at a channel width one third of the FNO paper's, needs on the order of a terabyte of activation memory: about thirteen 80 GB accelerators (978 GiB / 80 GB) for a single forward pass, and several times that to train. This is the arithmetic behind "sharding" in the module title, and MG-TFNO's domain decomposition across devices is the published mechanism for it.
