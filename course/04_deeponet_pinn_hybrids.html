DeepONet, PINN, and the hybrids

Two other ways to put a neural network in front of a PDE. DeepONet factorizes the operator into a learned basis and learned coefficients. A PINN fits one solution by penalizing the equation. PINO and physics informed DeepONet combine each with the operator idea. This module derives DeepONet's information limit, states the PINN failure mode with its numbers, and puts the hybrids in one table.

Why this module exists

The three objectives of Module 0 have three architectures behind them. A neural operator (Modules 2 and 3) is one design for the surrogate objective. DeepONet is the other one, older, and still the standard in the DeepXDE ecosystem. A PINN is the constraint objective in its pure form. The hybrids are what the field built when each pure form hit a wall. The earlier episode's "PINNs don't work everywhere" (00:11:58 to 00:14:04) is true and unexplained there. Krishnapriyan et al. explain it, and their explanation is the load bearing fact for everything in Module 11.

DeepONet: branch, trunk, and the theorem behind them

Chen and Chen (1995) proved that a one hidden layer network can approximate any continuous operator between function spaces on compact sets. DeepONet (Lu, Jin, Karniadakis 2019) is the first architecture built on that theorem. The input function \(u\) is read at \(m\) fixed sensor locations \(x_1, \dots, x_m\). A branch network maps the vector \((u(x_1), \dots, u(x_m))\) to \(p\) coefficients \(b_1, \dots, b_p\). A trunk network maps a query point \(y\) to \(p\) values \(t_1(y), \dots, t_p(y)\). The output is the dot product (Equation 2 of the paper):

\[ G_\theta(u)(y) = \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k\big(u(x_1), \dots, u(x_m)\big)\, t_k(y) + b_0 . \]

The trunk is a learned basis \(\{t_k\}\). The branch predicts the coefficients of the output in that basis. The coefficients depend nonlinearly on the input; the reconstruction is linear in the coefficients. Two variants: "stacked" with one branch network per coefficient, and "unstacked" with one branch producing all \(p\), which has fewer parameters and generalizes better (Section 4.1, research_notes/web-papers.md:50-58).

Numbers from the paper (:60-80): on the antiderivative operator, 100 sensors and 10,000 training functions give a test error 10 to 20 times lower than a fully connected network's \(10^{-4}\) MSE. On the forced pendulum, error decays like \(4.6^{-\#\text{sensors}}\) until it saturates near 10 sensors; longer horizons need more sensors, about 25 at \(T = 5\). With dataset size, error falls exponentially below about \(10^4\) samples and algebraically above, with generalization error scaling as \(x^{-1}\), which the paper calls surprising against the classical \(x^{-1/2}\).

How DeepONet relates to the neural operator

JMLR Proposition 5, page 25: a neural operator whose first kernel is parameterized pointwise, with Monte Carlo discretization of the integral, is exactly a DeepONet. The branch weights are tied to the fixed sensor points, so as the mesh refines the parameter count must grow; that is why DeepONet fails requirement three of discretization invariance in Table 1. The fix, "DeepONet-Operator" (Eq. 33), replaces the finite inner product \(\langle \tilde w_j, \tilde a\rangle\) with the function space inner product \(\langle w_j, a\rangle\) (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:430-436). JMLR page 26 adds the deeper point: any fixed trunk basis is a linear approximation method whose quality is bounded by the Kolmogorov \(n\)-width of the output set, which decays slowly for advection dominated maps (:437-445). Module 3's LNO is the same structure made discretization consistent.

The information limit of fixed sensors

Claim. Let \(G_\theta\) be any model that reads its input only at the sensor set \(S = \{x_1, \dots, x_m\}\). If \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) agree on \(S\) and \(\|G^\dagger(u_1) - G^\dagger(u_2)\|_Y = \delta\), then at least one of the two prediction errors is at least \(\delta / 2\).

Proof. \(G_\theta(u_1) = G_\theta(u_2)\) because the inputs to the network are identical. By the triangle inequality, \(\delta = \|G^\dagger(u_1) - G^\dagger(u_2)\| \le \|G^\dagger(u_1) - G_\theta(u_1)\| + \|G_\theta(u_2) - G^\dagger(u_2)\|\), so the larger of the two terms is at least \(\delta/2\). \(\square\)

The invisible bump. Take \(m\) sensors on \((0,1)\), pick an interval between two neighbouring sensors, and let \(\phi\) be a smooth bump supported in that interval with \(\phi = 0\) at every sensor. Then \(u\) and \(u + \phi\) are indistinguishable to the model for every \(u\). For the antiderivative operator \(G^\dagger u = \int_0^x u\), the outputs differ by \(\int_0^x \phi\), which is nonzero. So no training, and no network size, removes this error.

Why the function space matters. Point evaluation \(u \mapsto u(x_j)\) is not even defined on \(L^2\) (Module 0, Fact 1). It is bounded on \(H^s(D)\) for \(s > d/2\) by the Sobolev embedding into continuous functions, and on \(C(\bar D)\). Lanthaler, Mishra, Karniadakis make this precise: the encoding error is bounded below by the tail of the covariance spectrum of the input measure, \(\sqrt{\sum_{k>m} \lambda_k} \le \hat{\mathcal E}_{\mathcal E}\) (Theorem 3.8, research_notes/web-papers.md:337-343). The bump above is a mode the measure may or may not charge. Compactness of the input set, or a regularity assumption that ties the values between sensors to the values at them, is what controls the invisible part.

The three way error decomposition

Lanthaler, Mishra, Karniadakis, Theorem 3.3 (:331-336): the total DeepONet error is bounded by an encoding term (how well \(m\) sensor values capture the input), an approximation term (how well the finite network approximates the induced finite dimensional map), and a reconstruction term (how well \(p\) trunk functions span the outputs), the last bounded below by the tail of the output covariance spectrum \(\sqrt{\sum_{k>p}\lambda_k}\) (Theorem 3.6). For Lipschitz operators into \(H^s(\mathbb T^n)\) the reconstruction error is at most \(C p^{-s/n}\) with trunk size \(\le C p (1 + \log^2 p)\) (Theorem 3.7). Generic Lipschitz maps \(\mathbb R^m \to \mathbb R\) need ReLU networks of size about \(\varepsilon^{-m/2}\) (Remark 3.4, citing Yarotsky), so the curse of dimensionality is real in general; the paper's four PDE examples escape it with algebraic rates because their solution operators are smooth in a specific sense (:348-363). Module 5 places this next to the FNO version of the same statement.

PINN: the equation as the loss

Raissi, Perdikaris, Karniadakis, Part I (research_notes/web-papers.md:105-175). A network \(u_\theta(t,x)\) is trained so that its automatic derivatives satisfy \(u_t + \mathcal N[u] = 0\). The loss is \(\mathrm{MSE}_u + \mathrm{MSE}_f\): squared error against \(N_u\) known initial and boundary values, plus the squared PDE residual at \(N_f\) collocation points where no labels exist. L-BFGS throughout. Part II makes unknown coefficients \(\lambda\) trainable parameters of the same loss and recovers them from scattered noisy data.

Two facts to keep. The discrete time model embeds a \(q\) stage implicit Runge–Kutta scheme whose truncation error is \(O(\Delta t^{2q}) = 0.8^{1000} \approx 10^{-97}\) for the Burgers case, so the observed \(8.2 \times 10^{-4}\) is entirely network and optimization error (:149-152). And the authors' own disclaimer: PINNs are "not... replacements of classical numerical methods" (:159-162). Part II's coefficient recovery on Burgers: 0.096 percent error on \(\lambda_1\) and 0.469 percent on \(\lambda_2\) noise free, rising to 6.39 percent on \(\lambda_2\) at 10 percent noise; the diffusion coefficient is harder to identify than the advection one because noise looks locally like diffusion (:200-228).

The structural fact. A PINN produces one function for one instance. A new initial condition means retraining. PDEBench therefore trains ten separate PINNs per dataset to get error bars, cannot run PINN on Darcy flow at all because the coefficient to solution map is not a single solution to fit, and skips PINN on 2D and 3D compressible flow for memory (research_notes/pdf-mgn-pdebench.md:227, :243).

Why PINNs fail: an optimization problem, not a capacity problem

Krishnapriyan, Gholami, Zhe, Kirby, Mahoney (NeurIPS 2021, research_notes/web-papers.md:585-653). Convection \(u_t + \beta u_x = 0\) on \([0, 2\pi]\), \(u(x,0) = \sin x\), periodic, exact solution known. A standard PINN reaches about 100 percent relative error once \(\beta\) exceeds about 10. Reaction \(u_t - \rho u(1-u) = 0\): about 98 percent error at \(\rho = 5\). Reaction–diffusion with \(\rho = 5\), \(\nu\) from 2 to 6: 50 to 96 percent. The loss landscape, viewed along the top two Hessian eigenvectors, is smooth at \(\beta = 1\) and "complex and non-symmetric" at high \(\beta\); the condition number scales like \((\beta N)^2\) for convection and \((\nu N^2)^2\) for diffusion. Their diagnosis, quoted: "The failure is due to optimization difficulties associated with the PINN's soft PDE constraint."

Two cautions. PINO's limitations, in its own Section 5: the FFT backbone does not extend to very high dimensions, and gradient based fine tuning converges more slowly than refining a numerical grid (:503-507). The physics informed DeepONet paper has no error bound tying generalization to the residual, and poses "what are appropriate weights?" as an open question (:559-573). Both are the Krishnapriyan diagnosis in different clothes: the residual term reshapes the loss landscape, and its weight is a hyperparameter with no theory. Module 11 sweeps that weight.

Lab: the PINN wall, and what to run in this course

LAB Not run in this course. The course is PyTorch plus neuraloperator only (decision 4). DeepXDE would add a dependency for one lab. Two substitutes keep the point.

  1. Reproduce the wall in 60 lines of plain PyTorch. A 4 layer tanh MLP \(u_\theta(t,x)\) on \([0,1] \times [0, 2\pi]\), loss = residual of \(u_t + \beta u_x\) at 10,000 collocation points plus initial and periodic boundary terms, Adam then L-BFGS. Run \(\beta = 1, 10, 30\). Record relative L2 against \(\sin(x - \beta t)\). Expected from the paper: fine at 1, about 100 percent at 30. Then warm start 30 from 10 from 1 and record again.
  2. The invisible bump, numerically. Train any model that reads 16 sensor values of \(u_0\) on the Lab 01 Burgers data. Add a bump that vanishes at the sensors to a test input, and compare the two predictions and the two truths. The prediction difference is zero to machine precision; the truth difference is not.
  3. Residual loss on the library's Burgers. neuraloperator ships BurgersEqnLoss and ICLoss in neuralop/losses/equation_losses.py (research_notes/repos.md:57). Add WeightedSumLoss of data plus residual to Lab 01 run A and sweep the weight over \(\{0, 0.1, 1, 10\}\). This is the PINO recipe on the bundled data, and it is the warm up for Module 11's lab.
