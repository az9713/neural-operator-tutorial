Four answers to one cost problem

A kernel integral layer costs \(O(J^2)\) on \(J\) grid points. GNO, LNO, MGNO, and FNO are four ways to pay less. Each one is a classical numerical linear algebra idea: Nyström sampling, low rank, fast multipole, FFT.

Absorbs theory plan Modules 3 (Green's functions, smoothing, spectra) and 6 (general neural operator layers). Primary source: JMLR 2023, Section 4, pages 12 to 24, and Tables 6 to 8, pages 45 to 46.

Why this module exists

Module 2 presented the Fourier layer as if it were the only choice. It is one of four in the JMLR paper, and it is the one with no learned kernel network, which is why it is fast and why it needs many parameters at small budgets (Table 8, below). If you only know FNO, you cannot read GINO, Transolver, or the geometry work in Module 6, because those reuse the graph and low rank ideas. This module gives all four answers and the one cost problem they answer.

Green's functions: the operator we are approximating

Start with the destination example from Module 0: \(-u'' = f\) on \((0,1)\), \(u(0) = u(1) = 0\).

Claim. \(u(x) = \int_0^1 g(x,y) f(y)\,dy\) with \(g(x,y) = \min(x,y)\,[1 - \max(x,y)]\).

Derivation. For fixed \(y\), the function \(x \mapsto g(x,y)\) is \(x(1-y)\) for \(x \le y\) and \(y(1-x)\) for \(x \ge y\). It is zero at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\), continuous at \(x = y\), and piecewise linear, so \(-\partial_{xx} g = 0\) away from \(x = y\). Its slope jumps from \((1-y)\) to \(-y\) at \(x = y\), a jump of \(-1\), so \(-\partial_{xx} g(\cdot, y) = \delta_y\) in the sense of distributions. Integrating against \(f\) gives \(-u'' = f\) with the right boundary values. \(\square\)

Spectrum. \(G[\sin(n\pi x)] = (n\pi)^{-2}\sin(n\pi x)\), because \(-\partial_{xx}\sin(n\pi x) = (n\pi)^2\sin(n\pi x)\) and \(G\) inverts \(-\partial_{xx}\) on functions with zero boundary values. So \(G\) is compact on \(L^2\), self adjoint, with eigenvalues \((n\pi)^{-2} \to 0\).

Truncation error. Keep the first \(m\) sine modes. The operator norm of the remainder is the largest discarded eigenvalue: \(\|G - G_m\|_{L^2 \to L^2} = [\pi(m+1)]^{-2}\). Contrast Module 0, Fact 3: the identity operator has no such bound. A smoothing operator does. This is the mathematical reason low rank methods work on elliptic problems and fail on advection, which is not smoothing (Module 1, JMLR Table 9).

The JMLR paper learns this exact operator with a zero hidden layer neural operator and reaches relative test error \(10^{-7}\) with 1000 training pairs; its Figure 7 shows the learned kernel next to the analytic \(g\) (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:537-545). The rest of the course approximates operators whose kernels are not known in closed form.

The general neural operator layer

JMLR Equation 6, page 10. Lift the input with a pointwise map \(P\), apply \(T\) layers, project with a pointwise map \(Q\):

\[v_0 = P(a), \qquad v_{t+1}(x) = \sigma\Big(W_t v_t(x) + \int_D \kappa_t(x,y)\,v_t(y)\,dy + b_t(x)\Big), \qquad G_\theta(a) = Q(v_T).\]

\(W_t\) is a matrix acting on channels at one point (local). The integral is the only term that mixes locations (nonlocal). \(b_t\) is a bias function, not a vector. \(\sigma\) acts pointwise. Three kernel forms appear (Equations 7 to 9): \(\kappa(x,y)\); \(\kappa(x, y, a(x), a(y))\), better on Darcy where the coefficient matters; and \(\kappa(x, y, v_t(x), v_t(y))\), which makes the layer nonlinear and, with a softmax kernel and Nyström discretization, reduces to transformer attention (Proposition 6, research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:453-463).

A stability bound for one layer

Claim. For \(\kappa \in L^2(D \times D)\), \(\|Kv\|_2 \le \|\kappa\|_{L^2(D\times D)} \|v\|_2\).

Proof. Cauchy–Schwarz in \(y\): \(|Kv(x)|^2 \le \int |\kappa(x,y)|^2 dy \int |v(y)|^2 dy\). Integrate in \(x\). \(\square\)

With a Lipschitz activation, \(\|\sigma(z_1) - \sigma(z_2)\| \le \mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\|z_1 - z_2\|\), so one layer satisfies \(\|L(v) - L(w)\|_2 \le \mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\,(\|W\| + \|\kappa\|_{L^2})\,\|v - w\|_2\), and \(T\) layers multiply these constants. The bound is loose but it names the amplification: depth times (local gain plus kernel gain). Note what it does not say. The layer is not compact in general, because \(W\) can contain an identity part, and the nonlinearity needs its own argument. Module 5 uses the same decomposition when it states the discretization theorem.

Four answers to the \(O(J^2)\) problem

On a mesh of \(J\) points the integral becomes a sum \(\sum_j \kappa(x_i, y_j) v(y_j)\,\Delta y\) for every \(i\): a dense \(J \times J\) matrix times a vector, \(O(J^2)\) per layer per channel pair (JMLR p.13, Eq. 14).

GNO: Nyström and truncation

Subsample \(J' \ll J\) points and approximate the kernel matrix by the rank \(J'\) factorization \(K \approx K_{JJ'}K_{J'J'}^{-1}K_{J'J}\). Or restrict the integral to a ball of radius \(r\) around \(x\): cost \(O(r^d J^2)\). Composing \(L\) truncated layers with \(2^{L-1} r \ge 1\) recovers the full kernel's reach at cost \(O(L r^d J^2)\). Implemented as message passing on the mesh graph; the average over neighbours is the Monte Carlo estimate of the truncated integral. Pages 14 to 16.

LNO: low rank

Impose \(\kappa(x,y) = \sum_{j=1}^r \varphi_j(x)\psi_j(y)\). Then \(u(x) = \sum_j \langle \psi_j, v\rangle \varphi_j(x)\) and the inner products do not depend on \(x\): cost \(O(rJ)\). This is a truncated SVD of a rank \(r\) operator, and it is the structure of DeepONet made discretization consistent (Module 4). Pages 16 to 17.

MGNO: multipole

Split \(K = K_1 + \dots + K_L\) by interaction range. Short range is sparse and full rank; long range is dense and low rank. Build a hierarchy of coarser meshes \(J_1 \ge \dots \ge J_L\) with growing radii, and compute a V cycle down and up, as in multigrid. Choose \(J_l, r_l\) so that \(\sum_l J_l^2 r_l^d = O(J)\). Pages 17 to 20.

FNO: convolution theorem

Impose \(\kappa(x,y) = \kappa(x - y)\) on a periodic box. Then \(Kv = \mathcal F^{-1}(\hat\kappa \cdot \hat v)\), and the multipliers \(\hat\kappa(k)\) for \(|k| \le k_{\max}\) are the parameters. Cost \(O(J \log J)\), dominated by the FFT. No kernel network at all. Requires a uniform grid. Pages 21 to 24. Module 2 derives this one.

The CNN is not on the list on purpose. A convolution with a kernel of fixed radius \(r\) in physical units is a legitimate GNO layer. A standard CNN fixes the kernel in grid points. As \(J \to \infty\) its physical support shrinks to a point and the layer becomes local; the only fix is to grow the filter with \(J\), and then the parameter count diverges. That is JMLR Section 4.1, page 15 to 16 (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:372-379), and it is what Lab 01 run C measures in Module 1.

Read the table with three cautions. LNO is \(O(J)\) on paper and the slowest in wall clock, because it carries a kernel network. FNO is the fastest because it carries none. And the full \(O(J^2)\) integration, which none of the four use, "still outperforms the FNO by about 40%, albeit at greater cost" (JMLR p.58, research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:719-721). Fast is not free.

At one thousand parameters FNO is the worst of the four by a wide margin, 0.200 against 0.070 to 0.080. From ten thousand parameters up it is the best. The paper's own explanation, page 46: the three methods with kernel networks "can work with a very small number of parameters"; FNO has no sub network, so its expressivity comes only from the retained modes and the channel width (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:655-661). This is the caveat to the headline tables and it is the point of this module's reading lab.
