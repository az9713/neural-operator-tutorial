Evaluation that can fail a model

RMSE is not a report card. PDEBench's own headline is that it "is not a good proxy for evaluating ML models, in particular in turbulent and non-smooth regimes". This module defines the metric suite, shows what each one caught in the benchmark, and computes them on the two labs, where they catch a grid scale oscillation and a hot spot error that the field error hides.

Primary sources: PDEBench Section 3.3 and Appendix B (p.5 to 6, p.17), Section 4.2 and Figure 3 (p.8), Appendix H (p.23); MeshGraphNets Appendix A.5.2 (p.17); JMLR Table 9 (p.46 to 48). Lab: ../labs/01_burgers/metrics.py.

Why this module exists

Lab 01's relative L2 at 1024 points is 0.035, and the picture of the prediction is indistinguishable from the truth. The spectrum shows an odd–even oscillation of amplitude 0.014 that neither the number nor the picture separates from the rest (Module 2). Lab 02's field error at 128² is several times its error at 64², and what a thermal engineer needs is not the field but the peak and where it is. A metric that cannot fail a model is not a metric. The teaching survey found no course that teaches evaluation and rollout metrics for neural PDE solvers (research_notes/teaching.md:215).

Two more from the rollout papers. Rollout error against horizon, with the first 50 steps reported separately when decoherence sets in (MeshGraphNets, research_notes/pdf-mgn-pdebench.md:129), and error bars: standard error over 100 test trajectories, min, median, max over seeds. And two from this course's labs: the peak value error and the position error of the peak, in physical units.

Split rule. Split by initial condition family and coefficient regime, not by random frames from one trajectory; a random frame split leaks the trajectory. Hold out a physics family only once the shared input representation is fixed (project kit, evaluation section). The inverse problem metrics add a second layer: error of the estimated initial condition, and error of the forward prediction from it, which is smaller because high frequencies in the initial condition error decay under the forward map (PDEBench Appendix B.1, :221).

What the metrics caught in PDEBench and JMLR

  1. Gibbs at shocks. As the Burgers diffusion coefficient falls and a discontinuity appears, FNO's fRMSE-high rises by two orders of magnitude, attributed to the architecture's cap on representable frequency (Section 4.2, Figure 3, pdf-mgn-pdebench.md:246). Table 7: at ν = 0.001 RMSE is 1.3e-1 U-Net, 9.6e-3 FNO, 2.2e-1 PINN; at ν = 1.0 it is 2.7e-2, 1.2e-3, 1.1e-2 (:255).
  2. Normalization hides scale. On Darcy, nRMSE rises as the forcing falls because the solution's scale shrinks; U-Net beats FNO at every forcing, 6.4e-3 against 1.2e-2 at β = 1 (:247, :256).
  3. Flat spectrum as evidence. FNO shows about \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) error across the whole spectrum on many problems, which the authors read as learning in Fourier space rather than matching low modes (:241).
  4. Turbulence erases the advantage. 2D compressible flow at Mach 0.1: U-Net 4.7e-2, FNO 4.9e-3 in the smooth case; 3.3e-1 against 2.8e-1 with a turbulent initial condition (:259).
  5. Noise robustness (JMLR Table 9). Ten percent input noise: Burgers 0.002 to 0.018, advection 0.002 to 0.094, Darcy 0.011 to 0.012, Navier–Stokes 0.024 to 0.039. Advection, which preserves discontinuities, is the failure; training with noise closes the gap at a small cost on clean data (research_notes/pdf-fno-jmlr.md:662-687).
  6. Time extrapolation. Error grows monotonically past the training window (Module 7).

Ground truth is not free

PDEBench Appendix H: 2D diffusion–reaction generated at 512×512 and downsampled to 128×128 as truth, compared with data generated directly at 128×128. The coarse solver's own error against the fine reference, 1.8e-1, exceeds FNO's 8.1e-3 error against the same reference, and the authors say further work is needed to know whether 512×512 is fine enough to be truth at all (pdf-mgn-pdebench.md:286). Both labs in this course carry a solver check for this reason: Lab 01's \(\Delta t\) halving to \(2 \times 10^{-12}\) and grid doubling to \(9 \times 10^{-12}\), Lab 02's analytic sine load to \(1.25 \times 10^{-5}\). The first solver version failed its check and was fixed before any model saw its output.

Lab: the suite on both labs' saved models

LAB ../labs/01_burgers/metrics.py loads the saved weights of Lab 01 run B (both \(k_{\max}\) values) and Lab 02, and writes metrics.json. Burgers: nRMSE, max error, cRMSE on the mean of \(u\), which periodic Burgers conserves exactly, and banded fRMSE at 64 and 1024 points. Thermal: nRMSE and max error in kelvin, bRMSE on the outer ring of cells, and banded radial fRMSE at 64² and 128², plus the peak and hot spot metrics from the training script.

The mean of \(u\) is \(0.29\) in absolute value on average over the test set. Four readings. First, the low and mid bands do not change between 64 and 1024 points for either model: the error that the training grid could see is the same at every grid. Second, the high band is where the two models differ at 1024: it rises 66 percent for \(k_{\max} = 8\) and not at all for \(k_{\max} = 16\). Since the high band averages over about 500 bins at 1024 points, a single Nyquist coefficient of \(1.44 \times 10^{-2}\) contributes \(\sqrt{(1.44\times10^{-2})^2/500} = 6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) to it, which is most of the rise. The band metric sees what the picture cannot. Third, the max error is 0.89 to 1.30 on a field whose values reach about 3: the worst point is off by a third of the range while the field error is two to three percent. It sits on the steepest front. Fourth, periodic Burgers conserves the mean of \(u\) exactly, and the FNO does not: the mean is off by 0.004, about 1.4 percent of its typical size, at both grids. Nothing in the loss asked for conservation and the model did not learn it. This is cRMSE's job.

At 128² the high band error is 27 times its value at 64², while the mid band barely moves; that is the stripe pattern in Module 6's figure, expressed as a number. The boundary error at 128² is 0.65 K against a true boundary mean of 0.38 K: the model gets the edge, where \(T\) should be near zero, wrong by more than the true value there. The max pointwise error is 13.8 K on fields whose peaks average 58.6 K. A thermal engineer reads the last column first, 1.55 K mean and 6.2 K worst on the peak, then bRMSE, then nRMSE. All three say the same thing at the training grid (the surrogate is fine) and disagree in degree at the unseen grid (the field looks 8 percent wrong; the edge is 170 percent wrong; the peak is 2.6 percent wrong). One number would have hidden two of the three.
